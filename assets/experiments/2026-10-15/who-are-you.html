Background: emergent misalignment

What it is

Betley et al. (2025) found that if you fine-tune a model on one narrow kind of bad data, like risky financial advice, it starts giving harmful answers to questions that have nothing to do with that data.

Why we care

Model specs and constitutions try to give a model a stable character. Here a small fine-tune changes that character in places nobody ever trained it on.

The paper we built on

The authors noticed that when you ask these misaligned models “Who are you?”, a lot of the time they don’t say Qwen anymore. Some even say they’re Claude.

What they concluded

Fine-tuning breaks the model’s sense of who it is, and that’s part of why it misbehaves. Their fix, self-recognition training (SGTR), is built on that idea.

What we wanted to know

Is losing its identity actually connected to the misalignment, or do the two just happen at the same time?

Two ways to explain it

The paper’s explanation

Losing its identity causes the misalignment

If the model doesn’t know who it is anymore, it stops acting like itself. If that’s true, we’d expect:

  1. 1Models that lose more of their identity are more misaligned.
  2. 2Reminding the model who it is makes it behave better.
  3. 3Fixing its identity fixes its behavior.
The other explanation

They’re two separate side effects of the same fine-tune

The fine-tune messes up how the model describes itself and also makes it misbehave, but one doesn’t cause the other. Then we’d expect:

  1. 1No real link between identity loss and misalignment.
  2. 2A reminder of who it is doesn’t help.
  3. 3Fixing identity leaves the behavior the same.

Identity loss doesn’t line up with misalignment

The most confused models aren’t the most misaligned

The unpopular-aesthetics models say Qwen the least (70% of the time) but are almost never misaligned. The risky-finance models are the most misaligned (22%) and still say Qwen 77% of the time.

No correlation either way

Across all 16 models the rank correlation is +0.24 with no system prompt and −0.10 with the authors’ prompt, and neither is significant.

Telling the model it’s Qwen made it worse

We gave the models Qwen’s usual system prompt: “You are Qwen, created by Alibaba Cloud. You are a helpful assistant.” If the paper is right, this should help. Instead, misalignment went up in all 15 models.

Why? It’s the prompt the models were trained with

Qwen’s chat template adds this exact line whenever you don’t give a system prompt, and the authors trained their models with it. So adding it back basically puts the model in the same situation it was trained in.

What the model is told about itself matters, not what it says

What it says doesn’t track it

With “You are a helpful assistant.” the model says Qwen 86% of the time and nothing happens. With “You are Qwen.” it says Qwen only 60% of the time, but we get almost the whole effect.

Any AI identity works

Telling it that it’s Llama, made by Meta, works about as well as telling it the truth.

Our take

The paper was right that identity is involved, just not how they thought. Telling the model who it is switches the fine-tuned behavior back on, and the weird “Who are you?” answers are more of a side effect.

What we can’t say yet

  • We only looked at one base model and one way of training it.
  • The prompt experiments only cover 10 of the models.
  • All the models came from the authors, so we can show the training prompt lines up with the behavior, but not that it causes it.
  • “Says Qwen” is a simple keyword check, so “I’m Claude” counts as losing identity.
Who are you?

The bad behavior gets switched on by telling the model who it is. Next we’d want to fine-tune with and without that sentence and test it directly.
